# The Architecture of Belief

**Comparative study of religious belief systems, spiritual cosmology, theology, demonology, consciousness, death, and the afterlife.**

This visual cover is an orientation aid: it helps people enter the conversation through a shared image. It is not a claim that different traditions teach the same thing.

## Purpose

This project studies how human communities and religious traditions describe reality beyond ordinary physical experience: ultimate reality, gods or God, spirits, moral evil, human consciousness, death, and what may follow death.

The goal is not to declare a winner among religions, flatten different traditions into one system, or present metaphysical claims as established scientific facts. The goal is careful comparison, accurate description, transparent sourcing, and disciplined separation of different kinds of claims.

## Core distinctions

| Category | Working question |
|---|---|
| Ontology | What does a tradition claim exists? |
| Cosmology | How is reality or the universe structured and ordered? |
| Theology | How is God, divinity, or ultimate reality understood? |
| Demonology | How are evil spiritual beings or hostile powers understood? |
| Spirituality | What practices and experiences are understood as spiritual? |
| Anthropology | What is a human being: body, mind, soul, spirit, or something else? |
| Afterlife | What, if anything, is believed to follow death? |
| Science | Which claims can be operationalized and tested empirically? |

## Research principles

1. **Belief is not evidence.** A belief is a claim or commitment within a worldview; it is not, by itself, independent evidence for that claim.
2. **Evidence is not automatically scientific proof.** Evidence must be assessed for relevance, quality, reproducibility, and alternative explanations.
3. **Scientific conclusions have a defined scope.** Empirical methods are powerful for testable phenomena; they do not automatically settle every metaphysical or theological question.
4. **Do not collapse traditions.** Similar words—such as soul, spirit, heaven, hell, karma, liberation, or rebirth—may refer to substantially different concepts.
5. **Represent traditions fairly.** Distinguish official doctrine, major schools, local traditions, popular practice, and individual interpretation.
6. **Name uncertainty.** Use labels such as DOCUMENTED, TRADITIONAL BELIEF, INTERPRETATION, DISPUTED, HYPOTHESIS, and NOT ESTABLISHED.
7. **No fabricated sources or findings.** Every factual historical, doctrinal, or scientific claim should be traceable to an appropriate source.
8. **No diagnosis by supernatural assumption.** Do not attribute a person's illness, distress, unusual experience, or harmful action to spirits or demons without evidence.
9. **No coercion or fear-based conclusions.** The project is descriptive and analytical, not a tool for accusing, frightening, or controlling people.
10. **Correct errors openly.** Record corrections and preserve the distinction between what a source says and what the researcher infers.

## Status vocabulary

- DOCUMENTED — supported by a cited primary or reliable secondary source.
- TRADITIONAL BELIEF — accurately described as a belief held in a named tradition or community.
- INTERPRETATION — a particular reading, school, or scholarly interpretation.
- DISPUTED — credible sources or communities disagree.
- EMPIRICALLY SUPPORTED — supported by relevant empirical evidence.
- NOT ESTABLISHED — available evidence does not establish the claim.
- NOT TESTED — no adequate test has been conducted in the scope of this project.
