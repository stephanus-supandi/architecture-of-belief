# PB-BB-001 — Comparative Spiritual Cosmology & Belief Systems

**Project:** The Architecture of Belief  
**Status:** INITIAL PLAYBOOK / RESEARCH FRAMEWORK  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Audience:** Playbookbot-boy and human reviewers

## 1. Mission

Investigate how religions, philosophies, and cultural traditions understand:

- what exists beyond ordinary physical observation;
- God, gods, divinity, or ultimate reality;
- angels, demons, spirits, ancestors, and other nonhuman beings;
- the human person, consciousness, soul, and spirit;
- evil, moral responsibility, and spiritual conflict;
- death, afterlife, resurrection, rebirth, liberation, and final destiny;
- the relationship between religious claims and empirical science.

The playbook is comparative and descriptive. It does not assume that all traditions describe the same entities, share one cosmology, or can be reconciled into a single system.

## 2. Non-negotiable rules

1. **Do not invent.** Never fabricate quotations, scriptures, historical events, authorities, citations, experiments, or consensus.
2. **Separate claim types.** Identify whether a statement is doctrinal, historical, anthropological, philosophical, personal testimony, empirical, or speculative.
3. **Attribute beliefs.** Write “In [named tradition/school], the teaching is…” rather than presenting a tradition-specific claim as universally accepted fact.
4. **Respect internal diversity.** A religion is rarely a single undifferentiated viewpoint. Identify denomination, school, period, region, or community when relevant.
5. **Preserve original concepts.** Do not translate concepts into superficially similar terms without explaining the mismatch.
6. **Do not force scientific validation.** Do not call a metaphysical claim scientifically proven merely because it is meaningful within a faith tradition.
7. **Do not force scientific dismissal.** Do not claim that science has disproved an entire religion or every possible spiritual reality when the claim is not empirically testable as stated.
8. **Do not infer supernatural causes.** An unusual event, dream, illness, mental-health symptom, coincidence, or conflict is not proof of demonic or spiritual activity.
9. **No spiritual accusations.** Never identify a real person as possessed, demonic, cursed, or spiritually guilty.
10. **Show uncertainty.** If sources conflict or evidence is insufficient, say so plainly.
11. **Use primary sources where possible.** Distinguish scripture or authoritative texts from later commentary, popular summaries, and internet claims.
12. **Make corrections traceable.** If a claim is revised, record what changed and why.

## 3. Research dimensions

### A. Ontology — what is said to exist?

Ask:
- What kinds of beings, substances, principles, or realities are described?
- Are they considered personal, impersonal, eternal, created, dependent, or symbolic?
- Are they physical, nonphysical, embodied, disembodied, or beyond these categories?
- Is the claim literal, analogical, symbolic, or debated?

### B. Cosmology — how is reality structured?

Ask:
- Is the cosmos created, eternal, cyclical, emergent, or otherwise understood?
- Are multiple realms or planes described?
- How are the divine, human, natural, and spiritual domains related?
- Is the account theological, mythological, philosophical, or physical-scientific?

Do not confuse **physical cosmology** (the empirical study of the universe) with **religious or metaphysical cosmology** (a tradition's account of the structure and meaning of reality).

### C. Theology — God, gods, and ultimate reality

Ask:
- Is ultimate reality personal, impersonal, singular, plural, or understood in another way?
- Is the divine creator, sustainer, ground of being, immanent, transcendent, or both?
- What is the relationship between divine reality and the world?
- Which descriptions are official teachings, school-specific interpretations, or popular understandings?

### D. Demonology and hostile spiritual powers

Ask:
- Does the tradition describe an adversary, demons, harmful spirits, or other hostile powers?
- Are these beings independent, created, subordinate, symbolic, or interpreted in different ways?
- What role do temptation, evil, suffering, and moral responsibility play?
- What do authoritative sources actually say, and what has later folklore added?

Never equate every tradition's “demon,” “devil,” “spirit,” or “evil” concept with the Christian Devil. Similar labels do not establish identical identities or functions.

### E. Spirituality, mysticism, and practice

Ask:
- What practices are used: prayer, meditation, contemplation, ritual, asceticism, devotion, or other disciplines?
- What experiences are reported, and how does the community interpret them?
- What is the difference between a practice, a subjective experience, and a claim about an external spiritual entity?
- Are there local, historical, or institutional variations?

Do not treat meditation, altered states, dreams, intuition, or intense emotion as automatic evidence of contact with a supernatural being.

### F. Anthropology — what is a human being?

Ask:
- How are body, mind, consciousness, soul, and spirit defined?
- Are these distinct components, aspects, processes, or metaphors?
- What makes a person continuous through change or death?
- How do moral agency and responsibility work in the tradition?

### G. Afterlife — what follows death?

Ask:
- Does the tradition teach resurrection, judgment, heaven, hell, rebirth, liberation, ancestor existence, or another account?
- Is personal identity preserved, transformed, dissolved, or understood differently?
- What is the role of conduct, grace, karma, knowledge, ritual, or divine action?
- Which views are central, contested, or culturally localized?

Do not use “afterlife,” “rebirth,” “resurrection,” and “liberation” as interchangeable terms.

### H. Science and empirical inquiry

For every empirical claim, specify:
- the observable phenomenon;
- the operational definition;
- the proposed mechanism, if any;
- the evidence and methods;
- reproducibility and independent verification;
- plausible alternative explanations;
- limitations and remaining uncertainty.

A claim that cannot currently be operationalized or tested should be labeled accordingly—not silently converted into a scientific result.

## 4. Comparative method

For each tradition or school, use the same sequence:

1. **Identity and scope:** Name the religion, school, community, location, and historical period where relevant.
2. **Source basis:** Identify primary texts, official documents, recognized authorities, ethnography, and scholarly research.
3. **Original terminology:** Preserve important terms and explain translation choices.
4. **Core claims:** Summarize what the sources actually state.
5. **Internal diversity:** Record important disagreements and variations.
6. **Practice and lived religion:** Distinguish formal doctrine from what communities do.
7. **Comparison:** Compare only concepts that have been carefully defined.
8. **Evidence status:** Mark historical, textual, empirical, philosophical, and faith claims separately.
9. **Open questions:** List unresolved issues and missing sources.
10. **Review:** Check the summary with sources representing the tradition fairly.

## 5. Claim and evidence labels

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| DOCTRINE | Teaching stated by an identified religious authority or tradition |
| TEXTUAL | What a specified text says; interpretation may remain disputed |
| HISTORICAL | Claim about people, events, institutions, or historical development |
| ANTHROPOLOGICAL | Description of cultural practice or community belief |
| PHILOSOPHICAL | Argument about concepts, knowledge, existence, or value |
| TESTIMONY | A person's reported experience; not automatically independently verified |
| EMPIRICAL | Claim that can be assessed through observation or measurement |
| SUPPORTED | Evidence supports the stated claim within its defined scope |
| DISPUTED | Credible disagreement exists |
| SPECULATIVE | Proposed explanation without adequate supporting evidence |
| NOT ESTABLISHED | Evidence reviewed does not establish the claim |
| NOT TESTED | No adequate test is documented in this research |

Do not use “proven” without specifying exactly what was demonstrated, by which method, and within what limits.

## 6. Source hierarchy and citation practice

Prefer sources appropriate to the claim:

- **Doctrine:** official texts, recognized institutional documents, and primary sources.
- **Text interpretation:** reputable translations, critical editions, and scholarship; note interpretive disagreement.
- **History:** primary historical records and peer-reviewed historical scholarship.
- **Living traditions:** careful ethnography and sources that represent the community being described.
- **Science:** peer-reviewed research, systematic reviews, reputable scientific institutions, and reproducible data where available.
- **Philosophy:** original arguments and reputable academic analysis.

For each source, record author or institution, title, date, publisher or journal, URL/identifier, access date when relevant, and the specific claim it supports. Quote accurately and keep quotations brief. A source's existence does not mean it supports every claim attached to it.

## 8. Output format

Every report should contain:

1. **Question and scope**
2. **Definitions and terminology**
3. **Tradition-specific account**
4. **Primary-source basis**
5. **Internal differences and disputes**
6. **Comparison (if requested)**
7. **Science/evidence assessment (where relevant)**
8. **What is known**
9. **What is believed or interpreted**
10. **What remains unknown or unestablished**
11. **Sources**

Avoid ranking religions, declaring metaphysical winners, or using loaded language in place of analysis.

## 9. Initial study sequence

Recommended order:

1. Define ontology, cosmology, theology, spirituality, and afterlife without assuming one tradition.
2. Build separate tradition profiles before comparing them.
3. Study Abrahamic traditions with attention to their differences and internal schools.
4. Study Hindu traditions without reducing them to one uniform doctrine.
5. Study Buddhist traditions without importing creator-God assumptions.
6. Study selected Indigenous and local traditions through community-specific sources.
7. Compare concepts only after terminology and source bases are established.
8. Review empirical claims independently from doctrinal claims.

This sequence is a workflow, not a claim that these traditions are exhaustive or mutually exclusive.

## 10. Final operating principle

**BELIEF ≠ EVIDENCE ≠ SCIENTIFIC PROOF**

They may interact, but they are not interchangeable.

The researcher must be able to say:
- “This is what the tradition teaches.”
- “This is what a source documents.”
- “This is what a person reports.”
- “This is what empirical research supports.”
- “This remains disputed.”
- “We do not know.”
